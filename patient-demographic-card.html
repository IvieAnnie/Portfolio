01 — The Starting Point

I inherited a direction already in motion.

Patient Demographics is where a practice keeps who the patient is, how to reach them, and who pays for their care. Front desk staff check it at every visit. Billing and coding staff work in it for hours at a time, because every claim depends on getting it right.

When I returned to the company, the Patient Demographics redesign had already been designed and approved. I took over the project from my UX director as it moved toward development and release.

The redesign replaced a dense legacy demographics screen with a modular card framework intended to make patient information easier to organize and establish a reusable pattern across the patient chart.

Approved designLegacy screen
The approved design organized patient information into modular, collapsible cards.

02 — The Problem

The redesign didn't work for billing and coding users

Early beta sessions were encouraging. But the cohort skewed toward smaller practices and intake-focused workflows. We hadn't adequately tested the design with the billing and coding users who spend the most time in Patient Demographics.

When the redesign reached GA, those users had a “Back to Classic” switch that took them to the legacy screen, and they used it. Use of the switch peaked right after launch. Complaints about layout and usability became the loudest feedback on the release, and clients kept submitting ideas to change the card.

Going back wasn't a real option. The legacy screen was built on AngularJS, which no longer receives security updates, so it had to be retired. We were watching customer signals to know when we could start the countdown. As long as users kept switching back, that day couldn't come. The new card had to work for the people who lived in it all day.

So the job became clear: find the friction in their feedback and remove it, so people would stop switching back and adopt the new card.

The feedback pointed to seven friction points.

1

Too much hunting

Information that had been visible at a glance now required expanding and scrolling.

2

Not enough density

The cleaner presentation made it harder for experienced users to scan large amounts of patient information quickly.

3

A pop-up for every selection

Nearly every field opened a second-step pop-up, which broke keyboard-driven data entry.

4

Layouts that didn't stick

Cards didn't remember how each user left them, so people reopened the same sections every time.

5

Leaving the patient to check the schedule

Seeing the patient's next appointment meant navigating away from their information.

6

Slow patient lookup

Staff identify patients by chart number, but patient search didn't support it.

7

Names that didn't match

Names showed in a different format and order from the rest of the product, mixing title case with ALL CAPS.

03 — The Response

Research with billing, coding, and operations users

Rather than defend the redesign or simply revert to legacy, I went directly to the users whose workflows hadn't been adequately represented in beta.

I conducted nine structured sessions with billing, coding, and operations users, working through their actual workflows and testing possible changes as we went.

Nine structured sessions with billing, coding, and operations users.

04 — The Turning Point

Finding the layout fix during a research session

During one session, I started manipulating the cards with the user to understand what was making the information difficult to read.

When we reached a simpler single-column presentation, she found the information much easier to scan.

That gave me a direction: preserve the stronger grouping and readability, but use the available width to bring more information back into view.

I took the idea to our lead developer. The change was feasible without rebuilding the card, so we pushed for an iteration that introduced clearer labels and a two-column layout.

The two-column layout preserved the clearer information hierarchy while restoring the density experienced users needed.

05 — The Correction

The changes we shipped in response

We didn't throw away the card architecture. We focused the iteration on the problems research had exposed, and I designed every change in the package we shipped in response.

  • Two-column layout: density without the legacy form.
  • Original name convention restored.
  • Appointment mini card: the next visit, visible without leaving the patient.
  • Chart number in patient search: faster lookup the way staff already identify patients.
  • Sticky settings: cards remember how each user left them.
  • Type-ahead instead of pop-ups: no more second step for every selection.

The overall card direction stayed in place, but every section got its own detailed workflow design. We improved how each component worked. Where nearly every click used to open a second-step pop-up, fields are now type-ahead dropdowns that let users keep typing without breaking their flow. Each card section is designed around the task it supports, not a generic form.

06 — The Quiet Problem

Restoring the original name format

The loudest feedback was about density and layout, so that's where I focused research. But another issue kept surfacing in client ideas: how names were displayed.

The new card launched with a leadership decision to show names in title case and First Last order, which looks cleaner and more readable. The catch was in the data underneath. The legacy system had captured names in ALL CAPS for years, and a database of names can't be reliably converted to title case. Names like McArthur and MacDonald break. Clients ended up with a mix of title-case and all-caps names on the same screens, in a different order from the rest of the product.

As the feedback came in, I identified the pattern and brought it to our product owner, and we presented the evidence to the executive team: strong client voting and a clear workflow cost. We recommended returning to the original name convention. Leadership agreed, and the fix shipped in September 2025, restoring Last, First order and ALL CAPS–compatible names. It was the first change in the package to ship, ahead of the two-column layout.

07 — The Results

What changed after the fixes shipped

Layout and usability complaints, the loudest feedback at launch, fell to near zero. No ideas about the card have come in since March 2026. Support cases spiked twice as clients moved off the legacy screen, then settled below where they were before the transition.

Usage data showed the same thing. Comparing the same month a year apart, each user made about 61% fewer dead clicks and about 18% fewer clicks overall, and spent about three fewer minutes a day on patient information. That's an estimated 12+ hours a year back for each person. Finding a patient now takes about half the clicks it used to.

08 — The Lesson

What I learned from this project

The main lesson was that our beta hadn't included the people with the most demanding workflows.

Once those users were in the room, the problems became much easier to understand — and the solution didn't require throwing away the work we'd already done. The research also gave us the evidence to change a leadership decision.

Building the Patient Card meant creating a new card framework and workflow pattern, and the platform continues to build on it. The card framework became the foundation for what came next, including Patient Transaction History.
