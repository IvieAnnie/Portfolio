01 — The Problem

AI was arriving from several teams at once

AI features were being built in different parts of the product at the same time. Two product teams had each started designing an AI summary for their own screens. The clinical side was building a summary to prepare providers before a visit. Billing was planning AI that posts insurance payments.

The people using these features are careful for good reason. A wrong posting means a wrong patient balance and someone on the billing team cleaning it up. A wrong pre-visit summary puts a provider’s attention in the wrong place. They’re skeptical of anything that promises to “just handle it.”

If the AI explains itself one way in collections and another way in the chart, users have to rebuild their trust every time. With one pattern, they learn it once.

02 — The AI Features

Four features, one way of working

1

Collections summary

On request, summarizes the collections activity on a visit, with the last action taken and the next one to take. I designed it. Live.

2

Transaction History summary

The same summary, brought into the redesigned Transaction History instead of designed again. Coming with its beta.

3

Pre-visit summary

Prepares a provider for the visit with the chief complaint, vitals, and history. Designed by a designer I supervised, on the same pattern.

4

Payment posting

Reads insurance payment files (ERAs) and matches each payment to its claim, starting with the cases billers fix by hand most often. I designed it. In design.

03 — One Pattern

How every AI feature looks and behaves

I pulled the two teams designing summaries together and agreed on one design: the same placement, labels, states, and feedback. When the clinical pre-visit summary started, I supervised its designer and we built it on the same pattern. The wording changes for the domain, so clinicians are asked to review for “clinical accuracy,” but the parts and where they sit stay the same.

1

The AI mark and name

Every AI feature carries the same mark and says it’s AI in its name, so nobody mistakes generated text for something a person wrote.

2

A request to review

Every summary asks the user to check it. The wording fits the domain, but the place and the tone are the same.

3

Feedback beside the output

Thumbs up and down sit right next to the review request, in every feature, so giving feedback takes one click.

4

Runs only when asked

Summaries generate or update when someone asks for one, so the user decides when the AI works.

5

When it was generated

Each summary shows its time, so users know whether it reflects the latest activity.

Why the same feedback everywhere

The AI features are an add-on that practices subscribe to. Because every summary asks for feedback the same way, subscribing practices can tell us what’s working wherever they see AI output, and we get one signal we can compare across features instead of a different one per screen.

When the component library moved to standard Material 3, the summary moved with it, so the pattern stays the same as screens are modernized. That work is in UI Modernization.

04 — The Rules

Seven rules every AI feature follows

1

People choose how much the AI does

The practice decides, per payer, whether the AI only recommends or posts automatically. The on/off switch says in plain words what will happen: “Matching ERAs post automatically.”

2

Every line shows its status

Auto-posted with a confidence score, posting, pending, or an exception with the reason. Nothing happens off-screen.

3

Suggestions look like suggestions

When the AI isn’t sure, it says so, “AI suggestion — review before acting,” and shows the candidate visits so a biller can choose.

4

Anything the AI posts can be overridden

A biller can override any AI posting, and overrides and rule changes are logged. Control never moves out of reach.

5

Fail without side effects

If the AI service doesn’t respond, the work is left exactly as it was: “The claim line is unchanged — you can retry or work it manually.”

6

Say so when there isn’t enough to go on

If there isn’t enough new collections activity, the summary doesn’t stretch to fill the space. It says “Not enough new collections activity to summarize yet.” It’s the same rule I gave our research model: leave the gap instead of guessing.

7

Ask for feedback where the output is

Every summary is labeled as AI-generated, asks the user to review it, shows when it was generated, and has thumbs up and down right beside it.

The same four states in every summary

Each summary has four states, and each one tells the user exactly where they stand. These are the collections versions.

AI status on every payment line

Payment posting takes the same rules further, because the AI acts instead of summarizing. Every line carries its own AI status, so billers can see at a glance what posted, what’s in progress, and what needs them, along with the reason. Anything it can’t resolve goes to a queue with its reasoning.

05 — Feedback

How we’re gathering feedback and listening

The product team’s early assessment of the collections summary: a 75% reduction in manual note review time per claim, and more consistent collections follow-ups that no longer depend on which biller or staff member works the account.

Three signals will show whether trust keeps growing: the thumbs feedback on every summary, how often billers override what the AI posted, and whether practices move from “recommend only” to automatic posting over time. I also supported the technical product manager’s research.

What I learned

Consistency had to be someone’s job. Each team was making reasonable choices for its own screen, and nothing would have pulled them together without someone deciding that AI should look and behave one way across the product.

These features are also additions. When a practice doesn’t subscribe, they don’t appear, so every screen has to work with the AI and without it. That made adding AI to the screens people already use possible without waiting for a full redesign.
